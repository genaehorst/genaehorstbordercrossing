Into America: The Routes, Risks, and Realities of Immigration

Border crossings into the United States reveal complex human stories driven by survival and the search for safety, imploring us outsiders to really ask why.

By: Genae Horst

May 3, 2025 1:30 p.m. EST

Alexandria Bay Port of Entry in Jefferson County, New York. Northland Associates Inc., © 2019 Northland Associates, Inc.

The border. A term usually accompanied by thoughts of walls, illegality and well…our current President.

That being said, since January of 2024 rolled around, the number of people crossing from both the Mexican and the Canadian border have not slowed down. The number’s don’t lie. In fact, the amount of people crossing from all locations into the United States is stronger than ever, despite the massive risk. Shocking, I know. And yet, through various means of transportation - trains, buses, trucks, personal vehicles, railcars, containers or simply even walking- more and more people have flooded to the U.S.

These modes of transportation are the most commonly utilized by many seeking to enter the U.S. The two borders, the U.S.-Mexico border or the U.S.-Canadian borders are the most typical points of entry for those wishing to come into our country.

According to Iris Guzman, a law clerk at Legal Services of Central New York, there are a lot of people that come into the U.S. illegally. But it is not their desire to be illegal. “There's a lot of people that come here illegally because they have no choice, because they're fleeing their countries. But they want an avenue to get their citizenship, you know,” Guzman says.

The mode of transportation that the people Guzman tends to work with arrive in the U.S. in large trucks. She says that they describe it to her as hiding behind large boxes and traveling with coyotes.

Guzman, first generation daughter of immigrants from Nicaragua, says that a large number of Mexican people tend to immigrate into the Southern states while those from Cuba, South Korea and Ukraine tend to make their way up North, even to Syracuse.

When questioned about determining the status of one's legal or illegal identity when crossing the border, Guzman paused. Millions upon millions of people enter this country every year with just some level of paperwork to get you in. She says in the short term, there is no real way to determine if someone is legal or not because everyone is required to carry certain paperwork. But you cannot simply label someone as illegal without cause.

However, when looking at the data, some of the measures, i.e. the modes of transportation, were labeled “truck containers empty” or “rail containers loaded”. Guzman explained that when someone is illegally crossing the measure they cross in is usually nondescript. Something inconspicuous that does not draw a lot of attention. Things like buses and containers are most common because they have a lot of space but offer the most uncomfortable conditions.

Trains, personal vehicles, pedestrians all tend to be crossing legally as they are out in the open and no hiding is being done. Usually.

Robert Ratliff has spent 25 plus years in the immigration business. As a federal immigration court judge and now a federal and criminal immigration attorney he has practically seen it all.

Working with, almost exclusively, illegal immigrants, Ratliff relayed that the legal patterns have been accustomed to change since around the 1950s or so. “The thing that has changed most significantly since then is the countries where people are coming from, and the backlogs based on their countries of origin,” Ratliff said.

Most of the people Ratliff works with, much like Guzman, are illegally coming through the Southern border. He was quick to mention though that you do see a much smaller percentage, but a steady stream nonetheless of those crossing the Canadian border.

“You know, they either sneak through in a car or the trunk, coming back from Canada. There's a few places, upstate New York and Maine, where the US Canadian border runs right through the middle of town. So you get a few people that walk through there,” Ratliff said.

Many of these ports along the Canadian border exist in Upstate NY. In fact, there are six ports that see millions every year. A common mode of entry for the northern border is the ‘wave through’ entry. This occurs when the ports get too crowded so you have security officials standing at the border just waving people through. Funny enough, Ratliff tells me that this is still considered to be a legal form of entering the United States.

Despite the Canadian border being the less strict of the two, it is much larger. In geographical size, it is massive. But that also means that there are more places of entry leading into the U.S.. The border itself stretches some 5,000 miles, and those crossing tend to stay in the north, either exhausted from the journey and have no interest in going south or simply because the north has more sanctuary cities.

Iris Guzman says that Syracuse is a sanctuary city, and therefore illegal immigrants that make their way to Syracuse or Central NY in general are forthcoming about their status because they want to contribute to society. In Syracuse, immigrants are provided, well, sanctuary, and are protected legally from any threats.

Coming to understand the border and those who cross it is imperative, not solely for one’s own benefit but for the benefit of those who immigrate here. Crossing the border requires thoughtful contemplation and is accompanied with challenges some of us could never even imagine.

Knowing ports of entry are crucial to understanding the why of it all. By and large, the people that cross are not coming to cause harm or steal America’s money or take what is ‘rightfully ours,’ they seek a peaceful path to life in the United States.

They come to survive. To find safety and security in a place that supposedly ‘promises it all.’
